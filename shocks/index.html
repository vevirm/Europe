15 hypothetical external disruptions, with observed exposure distinguished from the imagined trigger. These are possible stress tests—not predictions or automatically refreshed scanner findings.
  1. A sudden overseas rare-earth licence freeze

    Hypothetical trigger: a major exporting authority suspends permits for a specific magnet-grade material after a diplomatic confrontation. Chinese supply concentration makes this worth stress-testing, but the study finds past coercion selective and does not predict a blanket embargo. Automotive motors and other equipment could be affected first; producers might bid against one another for inventories, passing costs to European buyers. Spare stocks, alternative grades, recycling and diplomacy could limit harm, though substitute qualification takes time. The severity depends on the exact material and the duration, in any year to 2035.

  2. A major overseas battery-technology supplier withdraws support

    Hypothetical trigger: geopolitical restrictions suddenly prevent a foreign technology owner from servicing production lines in European plants. The documented background is large foreign battery capabilities and proposed or announced investment in Europe; it does not prove such restrictions are planned. One plant might keep operating with existing stock, while another could lose software, spare parts or certified materials. Vehicle output and jobs would be exposed, but alternative equipment vendors, contract protections and emergency inventories could soften the blow. Effects would be highly firm-specific from now to 2035.

  3. A simultaneous interruption at two chip-chain chokepoints

    Hypothetical trigger: a geopolitical crisis interrupts Asian fabrication while a separate licensing decision delays European access to key equipment. Evidence establishes the international division of chip production and tensions around export controls, not the occurrence of a double shock. European vehicles, industrial controls and defence suppliers could face staggered shortages that cannot be solved by one new fab. Design flexibility, inventory, repair capacity and partner coordination would matter. The chain’s weakest specialised stage would determine severity. A possible 2027–2035 stress test, not a forecast.

  4. A large market threatens to exclude firms linked to one EU government

    Hypothetical trigger: a partner country announces informal import and procurement blacklists after a diplomatic decision by an EU Member State. The Lithuania case makes this mechanism conceivable, not inevitable. The pressure could cascade when international manufacturers ask European component suppliers to remove links to the targeted country. Legal challenge, EU solidarity and buyers’ willingness to accept substitution costs would determine whether coercion fails or spreads. The central uncertainty is whether the foreign market matters more to firms than their European supply ties. A near- to medium-term shock.

  5. An unexpected third-country tariff sends one sector toward Europe

    Hypothetical trigger: a major economy suddenly blocks a large category of advanced manufactured imports, prompting exporters to divert shipments to EU ports within weeks. The 2025 evidence supports diversion for high-exposure products, not a universal future wave. European producers could face price cuts and layoffs while downstream buyers benefit. Rapid injury investigations, temporary supply-chain adaptation and targeted safeguards could keep a sectoral shock manageable. The outcome depends on the goods, how easily firms can change destination and whether Europe can use the imports productively.

  6. A transit country suddenly becomes a large sanctions-diversion hub

    Hypothetical trigger: new controls reroute a sensitive product through a third-country trading centre that European customs had not previously treated as high risk. Earlier studies document sanctions-driven trade changes and some relative increases through intermediaries, not this future event. Exporters may face emergency documentation demands while forbidden end users try to exploit weak monitoring. Coordinated intelligence, targeted licences and diplomatic talks could stop the leakage without severing all trade. Severity depends on how quickly authorities distinguish evasion from legitimate commerce.

  7. A partner-country political turn freezes a newly relied-on trade route

    Hypothetical trigger: a country central to a recently expanded European trade arrangement changes export permissions after a domestic political crisis. Current evidence establishes new agreements and uneven implementation, not a forecast of withdrawal. Buyers who replaced one supplier with a single new partner might face shortages; a broader network with inventory could cope. Transparent dispute procedures and local partnerships would reduce the damage. The risk is particularly high if Europe calls a route diversified before businesses have tested alternatives. A medium-term scenario to 2035.

  8. A subsidy race leaves two countries with half-built strategic plants

    Hypothetical trigger: an overseas producer offers a high-profile factory to whichever European government provides the biggest incentive, then global demand collapses. Member States could compete, approve support and still have unfinished plants and weakened budgets. Research documents uneven aid geography, not this imagined auction. Joint assessment of demand, shared funding and clawback terms would limit losses. The shock would be fiscal and industrial rather than a blockade, with greatest exposure in regions depending on one project, sometime between 2027 and 2035.

    Hypothetical trigger: a security assessment finds a common externally supplied network component unacceptable and public buyers must replace it immediately. Hospitals, grids or transport systems could face delayed maintenance as suppliers scramble to qualify substitutes. Staged replacement, certified partners and emergency exceptions could reduce harm. The severity would reflect spare-part availability, cyber risk and whether the rule tests actual vulnerabilities rather than only origin, through 2032.

    Hypothetical trigger: after a political disagreement, an overseas owner announces it will stop funding a strategic European facility and withhold technical support. Local suppliers could lose orders even if the physical plant remains in Europe. Continuity covenants, diversified finance and replacement management might contain damage. The decisive question would be whether Europe can operate the asset independently and lawfully, a possible shock between 2027 and 2035.

The outcome depends on provider concentration and fallback rehearsal, anytime to 2035.

  14. A critical defence component becomes unavailable across allies

    Hypothetical trigger: a geopolitical crisis stops exports of a specialised component used in several European military platforms. The evidence points to varied national supply arrangements and the need for industrial integration, not a known future embargo. Countries might compete for the same remaining stock, delaying both new systems and repairs. Shared engineering specifications, qualified alternatives and pooled purchasing could reduce the damage. The shock would be sharpest if replacement requires lengthy safety certification. It is plausible during 2027–2035, with uncertain scale.

  15. A large exporter refuses EU carbon reporting and suspends steel sales

    Hypothetical trigger: a major steel-producing partner suspends certified shipments during a dispute over emissions verification. Early research on EU–India steel trade shows why this interface matters, but does not predict the imagined withdrawal. European construction and manufacturing buyers could face shortages or higher costs while domestic producers gain sales. Alternative verified suppliers, temporary inventory and technical negotiations could limit the shock. The key uncertainty is whether conflict concerns actual carbon performance or the rules for proving it. It could occur before 2035.
