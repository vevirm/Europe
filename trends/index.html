Mineral diversification is advancing faster on routes than on control

Europe is learning where the leverage sits

A 2026 study of gallium, magnesium, rare earths and vanadium finds Chinese supply control is concentrated but differs greatly by material. European researchers are also identifying which metals can realistically be recovered from old products. These are steps toward more precise protection, instead of treating every mineral as an equally urgent emergency.

Sources
  1. China’s mineral leverage is substantial but selective
    Energy Research & Social Science · 2026-10-29
  2. Ranking EU critical raw materials for recycling
    Resources Conservation and Recycling · 2026-09-19

New transport routes do not necessarily change who owns the mines

The study also finds that the existence of leverage is not the same as its frequent use. Research on the Angola–Congo–Zambia Lobito corridor warns that changing a shipping route may still leave mine ownership and processing concentrated. Recycling depends on collection and industrial-scale recovery, neither of which appears overnight.

Current balance: better diagnosis, but no demonstrated broad escape from concentrated supply. Watch actual refining capacity, ownership and recovery yields through 2028–2035. Results vary by material; a general shortage cannot be inferred.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

European battery investment is sought even as factory economics weaken

Domestic capacity remains an economic-security objective

The Centre for European Reform describes a thinning European battery factory pipeline and a policy case for keeping some production in Europe. Local cell-making could shorten supply chains and improve bargaining power over technologies used by vehicle makers and the electricity system. Firms are also exploring other chemistries and recycling as complements.

Sources
  1. How Europe could preserve a strategic battery industry
    Centre for European Reform · 2026-11-05
  2. Chinese battery investment and European ownership exposure
    Rhodium Group · 2026-11-05

Foreign capital can bring production but carry strategic ownership exposure

Rhodium reports announcements of Chinese investment in European vehicle and battery activities in the third quarter of 2026. Such investment can build jobs and know-how, but announced deals are not completed plants, and European buyers may still depend on foreign-owned technology or inputs. Research also identifies missing commercial links in lithium-iron-phosphate recycling.

Current balance: the case for local cells is clear, but the cost and investment gap remain material. Track operating output and contract terms, not announcements, during 2027–2035. Different battery types may produce different outcomes.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Europe is revising its chip ambitions while external licensing remains decisive

Chip policy is looking beyond factories

EU policy debate around a second Chips Act is turning toward skills, design firms, materials and the entire value chain rather than counting only new plants. An advisory opinion calls for financing, faster permission and support for smaller companies that design chips without owning factories. That is a broader view of what reliable European supply actually requires.

Sources
  1. Promising security, delivering dependency: the material constraints of EU semiconductor collective securitisation
    Journal of European Public Policy · 2026-08-01
  2. ASML becomes a test of European tech autonomy
    New Political Economy · 2026-10-30

Export controls and missing inputs still interrupt the chain

Research on Europe’s semiconductor strategy points to equipment, skills and material bottlenecks. An ASML case study shows the Netherlands balancing US security pressure against European commercial interests. Domestic fabrication does not eliminate the need for foreign specialist components or access to major markets, and the discussed Chips Act changes are not yet proven industrial results.

Current balance: policy diagnosis has improved more than verified capacity has. Measure delivered production, design services, qualified staff and licence predictability by 2030–2035; policy papers are not operating fabs.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Economic pressure is becoming a collective EU problem, not just a bilateral dispute

A small Member State can resist with broader support

Research on the 2021–2023 Lithuania-China dispute finds pressure on trade and multinational firms did not reverse Lithuania’s diplomatic position. It identifies European support, reputational costs and asymmetric exposure as limits on coercive leverage. That case matters because an individual country may have less room to absorb pressure than the larger European market.

EU actors still disagree about how much openness to preserve

A 2026 political-economy study argues Europe has no shared strategy that fully reconciles trade openness and security. A separate policy assessment documents simultaneous pressure from foreign economic leverage and weakening competitiveness. A single successful resistance case does not show every future target will withstand pressure or that every defensive instrument is cheap.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Trade barriers are redirecting goods, but the redirection is not universal

Some Chinese exports have moved toward Europe

An Economics Letters study finds that, among products most exposed to 2025 US tariffs, Chinese export quantities shipped to the EU rose relative to less-exposed products. This supports a narrow trade-diversion mechanism: when one large market becomes harder to reach, sellers search for another. European manufacturers can face extra competition as a result.

Sources
  1. US tariffs diverted some Chinese exports to the EU
    Economics Letters · 2026-09-15
  2. Distributional impacts of EU tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles across European markets
    Nature Energy · 2026-07-10

Tariffs can also raise European consumer and production costs

A Nature Energy study of tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles examines how carmakers and consumers respond differently across European markets. Trade-defence proposals seek quicker action against damaging import surges, but the research does not show that all imports surged or that every protective tariff helps the European economy. Affordable imported inputs and competitive prices can be benefits of openness.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Russia sanctions have changed trade while enforcement still differs by state

Trade restrictions have altered established commercial routes

A study of 2013–2024 Central European trade finds particularly large changes in machinery exports to Russia after sanctions and later changes in energy imports. A November 2026 European Court of Auditors report also records a sharp fall in EU–Russia goods trade since 2021. These developments show that sanctions operate on real supply chains.

Circumvention and uneven enforcement blunt the effect

The European Court of Auditors identifies differences in national enforcement and gaps in Commission monitoring. Separate econometric research finds relative increases in selected sanctioned-goods exports to Russia from Kazakhstan and Türkiye. That evidence shows a possible rerouting channel; it does not mean every such trade flow violated a specific EU rule.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Europe is widening trade routes faster than some partnerships become operational

Market-opening agreements are creating formal options

The EU–Mercosur interim agreement entered provisional application in May 2026, according to its official publication record. EU analysts and researchers are also exploring deeper economic ties with India and Southeast Europe. Wider routes can give European buyers and exporters alternatives when existing markets become politically risky.

Formal agreements do not guarantee practical supply chains

An Egmont assessment finds the EU–India package still lacks important implementation steps, while peer-reviewed analysis finds cooperation more advanced in maritime and digital areas than in binding market integration. The Southeast Europe study argues for investment and integration but reports a recommendation, not completed projects. Standards, border capacity, infrastructure and business confidence still matter.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Industrial policy is expanding through budgets that are not equally available

National governments are using state aid for shared industrial aims

Institutional analysis shows that national subsidies increasingly serve EU-wide technology and production objectives. Projects of Common European Interest provide one way for countries to coordinate investment in strategic sectors. In principle, pooling projects can help create supply chains too expensive for one company.

Richer Member States can finance more of those ambitions

A comparative study of eleven shared-interest projects finds funding concentrated in core economies even where peripheral countries participate. The legal analysis points to fragmented common fiscal capacity. A 2026 EU audit of earlier innovation funding also warns that supporting research is not the same as proving commercial uptake. National flexibility can undermine a fair single market.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Public procurement is becoming a security tool, but its costs remain contested

Buyers are considering who supplies strategic goods

A Research Policy study links security assessments in 1,613 procurement projects with favourable innovation and critical-technology outcomes specifically where domestic suppliers won. Meanwhile, EU officials are discussing whether public funding should prefer goods made in the Union or in qualifying partner countries.

Preference rules can narrow competition and provoke trading partners

The 2026 procurement reform and industrial-origin documents discuss proposals, not rules already operating throughout the EU. Restricting eligible suppliers may raise costs or lose access to specialised partners. In the procurement study, the apparent innovation benefit was conditional on domestic awards; it was not a universal improvement or a demonstrated environmental gain.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Investment screening is spreading while countries still measure it differently

More transactions face security examination

A CELIS comparison reports more national investment-screening filings in 2025 than 2024, while warning that countries count them differently. The revised EU framework provides for wider shared risk analysis, including a future Commission database of sector and investor assessments. These changes could help spot acquisitions involving critical facilities or sensitive know-how.

Sources
  1. EU investment screening grew faster than headline ratios suggest
    CELIS Institute · 2026-11-09
  2. EU FDI risk database can inform future national screening
    CELIS Institute · 2026-10-22

Review cannot replace productive foreign investment

The legal analysis says Member States are not automatically bound by future Commission assessments, and the database provisions are scheduled to apply in 2028. Screening can impose uncertainty on companies and deter partners if tests are too broad. The comparative filing count cannot, by itself, establish that investment became more dangerous or that decisions became more effective.

Europe is seeking more control of digital services without leaving global networks

Financial and public users are identifying external bottlenecks

The European Central Bank has warned that reliance on foreign advanced AI models could transmit access or cyber shocks into finance. European payment schemes announced a joint network to reduce reliance on US providers, and Portugal’s Digital Decade report identifies cloud-adoption gaps while describing sovereign infrastructure ambitions.

Domestic replacements have not yet proved equal reach or capability

The payments arrangement is an announced network, not evidence of mass merchant adoption. The ECB described a brief US AI access restriction but did not report a financial-system disruption from it. European technology can improve resilience only if users can obtain competitive, interoperable services; global providers still supply innovation and scale.

Europe is reducing one energy exposure while weighing new import dependencies

The move away from Russian gas changes the security equation

The Commission’s June 2025 proposal sought a phased end to Russian gas imports and better monitoring of dependence. New research examines EU support for lower-carbon hydrogen and cross-border networks, showing a policy effort to build other energy and industrial options rather than simply replace one gas supplier with another.

None of these studies proves full-scale replacement energy has arrived.

Real disruptions are revealing the links between networks

The European Environment Agency documents 2026 weather disruptions affecting freight, power and telecommunications, including links between sectors. A port study compares the different critical assets of Seville, Lisbon and Dunkirk and finds that utilities, drainage and access routes often matter as much as docks themselves.

Research on critical rail cybersecurity finds demanding sector-specific implementation needs, while reporting on European grid protection identifies unequal readiness. The port paper tests vulnerabilities rather than proving that all three ports failed. A uniform infrastructure checklist cannot solve every local threat, and duplicating everything would be expensive.

European defence cooperation is growing, while supply decisions remain national

Funding eligibility and producer geography are expanding

Ukraine’s association with European Defence Fund and defence-industry funding became effective in October 2026, creating a legal route to joint work. Research also finds a longer-term increase in higher-value defence exports by Central and Eastern European manufacturers. Together these developments broaden the potential European production base.

Work on Central and Eastern European supply chains shows why industrial integration is difficult. Eligibility for EU funding does not prove a factory has expanded or that common procurement and equipment delivery have followed.

Direction and counterforce · 2026–2035

Carbon-linked trade rules are expanding as partners question their costs

Europe is using emissions accounting in market access

Research examines early shifts in EU–India steel trade linked to the carbon border adjustment mechanism, which charges for emissions in selected imports. A legal study proposes recognising credible overseas verification to reduce administrative friction without abandoning emissions standards. The policy aim is to keep greener European producers from being undercut by dirtier production.

Sources
  1. CBAM verification and mutual recognition
    Journal of International Economic Law · 2026-09-26
  2. Early signs that the EU carbon border adjustment mechanism is reshaping EU–India steel trade
    Nature Climate Change · 2026-06-08

Different accounting and industrial costs can hinder cooperation

The EU–India study offers early signs, not a definitive measure of every sector’s response. Carbon-verification requirements can be difficult for smaller foreign firms, while modelling of industrial precursors suggests importing some low-carbon inputs may still be cheaper than local production. Tightening rules could create trade disputes unless equivalence is demonstrated fairly.
