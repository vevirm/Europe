15 enduring economic-security mechanisms. Each explains what sustains it, whose choices it shapes and what could change it. Manually selected and fixed until the next publication.

  • The bottleneck is often processing, not simply the ground beneath a mine

    European manufacturers need usable metal, not merely an overseas mining agreement. A resource can travel along several routes and still pass through one dominant processor or owner before reaching a European factory. The four-material study shows why leverage is specific to each material. A modelling study of magnets suggests recovered metals can help later, but only if products are collected, separated and processed efficiently. This structure persists because mines, refineries, contracts and specialist skills take years to build. It affects motors, clean-energy equipment and defence procurement. It would change if independent processing and verified recycling scaled up; progress is not yet the same as replacement.

  • Battery security is a chain of chemistry, equipment and ownership

    A factory physically in the European Union does not automatically give European firms control over its technology, upstream materials or future operating decisions. The CER assessment highlights cancelled projects and Chinese dominance; Rhodium records announced Chinese investments that could add local capacity. Recycling research finds a further missing step: a chain that converts used batteries into commercially usable inputs. These layers reinforce one another because manufacturers need affordable materials and reliable technology to justify production. The pattern affects vehicle workers, energy-storage buyers, lenders and foreign investors. It could change with competitive plants and diversified contracts, but policy cannot decree commercial viability.

  • A chip can be European at one stage and externally dependent at another

    Chips require design software, equipment, materials, manufacturing and advanced packaging, often in different countries. The research on European chip policy argues that announcing production does not supply the missing materials and workforce. The Dutch ASML case shows how another state’s export-control choices can affect a European company even when technology is made in Europe. This is a lasting condition because each stage requires highly specialised investment and learning. It affects industrial automation, communications and defence. Cooperation is productive as well as risky; fully recreating the chain would cost far more than targeting the most dangerous gaps.

  • Companies can be pressured through a country they do not belong to

    Economic coercion can target a government indirectly by warning international firms that sourcing from, or selling through, that country threatens their larger market access. The Lithuania study identifies exactly this cross-border channel. It persists because European suppliers operate within world-wide production networks, while state-level security decisions and commercial choices are made by different actors. Common EU tools and coordinated diplomacy can reduce isolation, but a policy that shuts every contested relationship could destroy productive cooperation. The phenomenon is about bargaining power inside networks, not a claim that every dependency is weaponised.

  • The EU is a second destination for trade displaced elsewhere

    Exporters do not stop selling when one country raises a tariff: they can cut prices, redirect particular goods, or build elsewhere. The product-level 2025 US tariff study documents redirection toward Europe in the most exposed group, but not the wider basket. That distinction matters because European consumers, factories and importers occupy different places in supply chains. It persists while large external economies adopt changing restrictions and Europe remains an attractive market. European firms can benefit from cheaper inputs even while others lose market share. Monitoring must therefore compare individual sectors and whether alleged injury has actually occurred.

  • A common sanctions list is enforced through separate national systems

    EU governments agree the overall sanctions framework, but customs agencies, investigators, licensing offices and courts across Member States enforce it. The 2026 Court of Auditors report documents uneven practices and monitoring gaps. The 2013–2024 trade study shows how restrictions change actual flows, while research on neutral states shows how some trade shifts through other jurisdictions. This pattern persists because trade networks adapt faster than separate public administrations harmonise their data and capacity. It affects legitimate exporters too: unclear enforcement makes compliance costly. Better sharing could improve security without treating every re-export as unlawful.

  • An agreement on paper is not yet a working replacement supplier

    European trade agreements open legal doors, but reliable supply also needs ports, customs, financiers, credible producers and stable rules. The EU–Mercosur agreement’s provisional application is a concrete institutional step, while assessments of EU–India cooperation identify significant implementation gaps. This two-speed process persists because trade negotiations are centrally conducted but firms and Member States make separate investment decisions. It affects food producers, industrial buyers and partner-country workers. Partners also pursue their own interests; diversification must remain reciprocal. Long-term resilience is earned through use of the agreement rather than counting signatures.

  • European industrial aims often depend on unequal national treasuries

    EU rules allow support for strategic projects, but the size and timing of grants still often depend on individual governments. Research on eleven projects finds aid concentrated in stronger economies, and legal analysis explains the absence of equally large common fiscal tools. Over time, suppliers may cluster around the best-funded locations, reinforcing differences within Europe even if total EU capacity rises. This affects smaller Member States, workers and small firms seeking entry to large projects. The effect may be softened when cross-border procurement and EU cofinancing spread production. The enduring tension is between fast national action and a competitive, integrated European market.

  • An ordinary public purchase can determine future strategic suppliers

    A hospital, grid operator or public agency may become the early customer that allows a domestic technology business to grow. Security-minded procurement can create this demand, as a study of European tenders suggests under particular contract conditions. But different public buyers have different budgets and legal duties, and a single-country preference can exclude trusted partners or innovative firms elsewhere in the Union. This enduring tension links market access, industrial learning and public service quality. It can change if procurement rewards verified security performance and delivery across an open European supplier base, rather than nationality alone.

  • Ownership can be a route to both capital and political influence

    Foreign investors can supply money, expertise and access to global sales. Ownership of a critical port, software platform or industrial plant can also determine how assets are operated, upgraded or sold during a political dispute. European screening rules exist because this difference matters. A 2026 comparison finds rising reported filings, but the record stresses measurement differences; forthcoming common risk assessments still leave national authorities responsible for decisions. This persists because economic and security benefits are assessed by different institutions and investors continually enter new technologies. It affects workers, local economies and national regulators. Better shared evidence could change review quality without closing Europe to capital.

  • Digital dependence is often an access right, not a visible imported box

    A European bank or administration can own its local computers while relying on software licences, foreign AI model access, identity systems or payment networks controlled elsewhere. The ECB’s 2026 warning explains how an access restriction could matter even without a broken cable; its reported episode caused no discernible financial disruption. European payments firms are assembling an alternative network, but take-up is unproven. This pattern persists because digital services benefit from scale, specialised expertise and network effects: users choose where other users already are. Building fallback routes can improve bargaining power while preserving useful international services.

  • Replacing a fuel can create a new dependency in industrial ingredients

    A steel or chemicals plant may need hydrogen, ammonia or processed iron rather than imported natural gas. Research suggests some foreign-made green precursors could be cheaper than producing everything domestically. That can help European competitiveness but shifts exposure to new suppliers, ports and transport contracts. At the same time, hydrogen network operators identify uncertain tariffs and investment costs. This structural trade-off persists because energy availability, industrial demand and infrastructure have to develop together. Security improves only when companies can change suppliers or technologies if one route fails; forcing domestic production at any price could itself undermine prosperity.

  • The weak link in a supply chain may be power, drainage or a control system

    Factories and ports depend on electricity, access roads, communications and software even if their own equipment is sound. The 2026 European Environment Agency review documents how real storms, drought and power constraints affect connected networks. Port researchers identify different weak assets across three ports; rail cybersecurity work adds the digital dimension. This persists because infrastructure was built by many operators at different times, with different budgets and responsibilities. A European trade corridor can therefore be exposed far from the border. Shared emergency planning can improve resilience without rebuilding every asset.

  • Defence demand is shared, but procurement and factories remain fragmented

    European forces need equipment that can be produced and repaired reliably. Yet governments retain distinct buying priorities and industry relationships, as defence-supply research documents. Central and Eastern European firms have capabilities, but their integration into large multinational orders depends on standards and demand. Ukraine’s 2026 eligibility for EU programmes opens a route to cooperation without guaranteeing delivered weapons or jobs. This tension persists because security preferences, budgets, export rules and skills vary by state. It can also create a strength: more producers offer more geographic resilience when their parts work together.

  • An emissions standard can protect climate goals and become a trade barrier

    When the EU charges imported products for their embodied carbon—emissions released in producing them—it asks overseas suppliers to measure and verify their factories. That can reward cleaner production and defend European investment. It can also exclude otherwise competitive firms lacking expensive verification systems. Early steel-trade research and legal analysis of mutual recognition illustrate the tension, while green-input models show the benefits of some imports. The pattern will persist as environmental objectives and open trade interact. It could become less divisive if trusted verification systems recognise equivalent measurement across countries without weakening standards.
