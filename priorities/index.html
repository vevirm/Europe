Risks

Developments that could leave Europe more exposed.

15 risks
  1. A mineral export licensing halt could stop European production downstream

    The documented starting condition is concentrated Chinese control of some mineral supply stages. If export licences become slower or narrower, European magnet and equipment makers could miss deliveries; customers might then stockpile, multiplying price spikes. A long interruption would matter more than a short one because substitutes need redesign and certification. The four-material evidence also warns against assuming all materials or Chinese measures are alike, and new routes or recycled stocks could absorb some disruption. This is a plausible 2027–2035 pathway, not an observed Europe-wide production shutdown.

  2. Rescuing a battery factory could lock buyers into a single foreign technology

    A European state might support a struggling cell plant to protect industrial capacity, then discover it still relies on one supplier’s equipment, chemistry or access to upgrades. The result could be a costly domestic site without dependable strategic control. Existing studies document a weak European project pipeline and the mixed role of foreign investment, not a confirmed takeover of the industry. Multi-supplier contracts, transparent ownership review and technology transfer can limit exposure. It is a medium-term risk for firms, taxpayers and car producers, especially if incentives overlook operating costs.

  3. An export-control mismatch could strand European chip investment

    Suppose a new fab is built in the EU but a foreign government changes licences for a tool or software update that the fab uses. Production may be delayed while financing costs continue, and European downstream customers could shift to overseas suppliers. The ASML policy study establishes cross-border licensing tensions; it does not report this particular future loss. Multiple equipment options, advance licensing talks and trusted-partner agreements could prevent a crisis. This risk is most acute during the lengthy build-and-qualification period from 2027 to 2035.

  4. A member-state dispute could become an EU-wide supplier boycott

    An external state might threaten large multinationals with loss of market access unless they stop buying inputs from a particular EU country. The first loss would hit local factories; cross-border contracts could then transmit it to firms elsewhere in the single market. Lithuania provides documented evidence of such tactics and also evidence that they did not achieve the intended reversal in that case. Collective support, customer switching and an effective common response could contain the risk. A different, larger market or a more essential component could change the outcome during 2027–2035.

  5. A rushed tariff response could protect one industry and damage another

    If displaced imports arrive rapidly, some EU factories could lose orders. A hasty blanket restriction may then make equipment or affordable vehicles dearer for other European users, delaying their investment. The observed trade-diversion study concerns a product subset; a broader surge is a possibility, not an established fact. EU trade-defence investigations, evidence of injury and targeted measures can limit overreaction. The risk is highest when trade policy moves faster than sector-specific evidence, with effects possible in 2027–2030.

  6. Sanctioned components could reach Russia through a trusted-looking intermediary

    A trader may redirect sensitive goods through a non-EU country with ordinary commercial paperwork. If EU enforcement agencies cannot compare end users and transit patterns, restricted items could reach prohibited buyers, weakening policy credibility and creating legal risk for unaware exporters. The Court of Auditors documents enforcement gaps and research identifies rerouted trade, not a specific new criminal case. Shared checks, company due diligence and targeted investigation are buffers. Over 2027–2030, the outcome hinges on identifying suspicious transactions without blocking lawful trade indiscriminately.

  7. A delayed trade partner could leave Europe with only nominal diversification

    An EU manufacturer may plan to replace a concentrated supplier using a new free-trade or strategic partnership, only to find that permits, origin certificates or transport capacity are not ready. If a disruption arrives before real trade flows diversify, the company is no safer despite the announcement. The EU–India analysis documents that frameworks and practical cooperation can diverge; it does not establish widespread future failures. Supplier trials, dual contracts and realistic delivery milestones are safeguards. The risk unfolds over years, especially if policymakers count agreements as achieved resilience.

    If several Member States bid for the same battery or chip factory, a firm might choose the largest public offer rather than the strongest supply-chain location. Europe could pay more without adding net capacity, while smaller countries lose skilled workers and suppliers. The observed geographical concentration of aid supports concern, not proof that a particular future auction will fail. Transparent common criteria, cross-border projects and commercial performance tests can reduce the risk. It could build over 2027–2035 when multiple large facilities seek public help.

Evidence shows such eligibility criteria are debated and that procurement benefits depend on contract context; it does not prove a damaging final rule. Carefully written exemptions, competition checks and performance-based security tests would limit the danger. The mechanism matters in 2027–2032 as proposals become actual contracts.

  11. Loss of access to one AI provider could spread through financial operations

    Suppose a foreign model provider withdraws updates or access to European banks during a trade dispute. Shared automated workflows could degrade at the same time and a rushed substitute might produce operational errors. The ECB identifies this type of dependency and cyber coupling, while its example of a brief restriction had no proven systemic consequence. Local fallbacks, multi-provider testing and human oversight are buffers. The risk is not that all AI is unsafe, but that concentration can turn a licensing decision into a correlated disruption, increasingly relevant by 2030.

    If governments finance pipelines assuming rapid hydrogen demand, but complex support rules and high equipment costs delay projects, network charges could rise on a small customer base. Energy-intensive factories would then face higher costs and defer investment, weakening industrial resilience. Operator surveys and comparative policy research establish investment barriers, not an observed EU-wide stranded pipeline. Gradual buildout, flexible use and contracts tied to real demand offer safeguards. The exposure grows from 2027 into the 2030s as infrastructure decisions become hard to reverse.

    Floods or low river levels could restrict freight while grid damage limits warehouses and rail signalling. Firms relying on just-in-time imports might then miss production inputs and divert traffic into congested routes, multiplying the losses. The 2026 Environment Agency analysis documents real cross-network effects; the imagined simultaneous continent-wide failure is not an established fact. Backup energy, alternative freight paths and coordinated restoration plans provide buffers. The risk will grow where adaptation lags changing hazards, but geography and seasons determine exposure.

Research establishes differences in procurement concepts and industrial integration; it does not prove an imminent EU-wide production failure. Shared specifications, joint long-term orders and backup component suppliers can reduce risk. It matters through 2027–2035 as demand and capacity are matched.

Opportunities

Developments that could give Europe more room to act.

15 opportunities
  1. Build usable European magnet feedstock from returned equipment

    European automakers, recyclers and public buyers could contract for the collection of spent motors and separate their rare-earth magnets, then guarantee purchases of qualified recycled material. A European modelling study tests why collection and product design matter, while the material-priority ranking identifies where recovery is strategically valuable. The gain would be a second, locally available source—not complete self-sufficiency. Plants require financing, reliable scrap, low-waste chemistry and years of qualification. If recycling proves costlier or environmentally worse than expected, procurement should adjust rather than guarantee demand blindly. A practical 2027–2035 opportunity.

  2. Use joint orders to create a market for alternative battery cells

    European carmakers and grid-storage buyers could combine predictable long-term orders for European and trusted-partner battery chemistries that meet performance standards. Orders would let suppliers finance equipment and qualify cells, while diversified chemistry reduces reliance on a single production ecosystem. Existing research identifies the exposed European pipeline and recycling gaps, so this is a response to a specific bottleneck rather than a claim of success. The obstacle is price: forcing expensive cells on buyers could damage electric-car adoption. Contracts should reward delivery and affordability, with exit clauses. The opening runs to the early 2030s.

    The Commission, Member States and private chip firms could map the stages where a single supplier truly blocks production, then co-invest selectively in packaging, design tools, repair skills and specialist materials. The second-Chips-Act discussion and structural research point to those gaps, but only delivered services and customers would show success. Keeping research partnerships and overseas fabrication for less sensitive uses would avoid a very costly attempt at total self-sufficiency. Shared EU financing and open eligibility for smaller firms are needed to avoid concentrating gains in a few large states. This is a 2027–2035 opportunity.

The Lithuania case suggests cohesion and alternatives can weaken pressure; the lack of a single settled strategy warns against assuming an instrument alone is enough. Help would work only if businesses trust confidentiality, replacement contracts are commercially viable and EU action is coordinated. It must avoid punishing ordinary trade with reliable partners. This could reduce the leverage available to a coercer without requiring broad decoupling over 2027–2030.

  5. Design trade responses around measurable product harm

    The Commission could link customs and production data to quick, published checks of whether a diverted product is actually injuring EU producers. Where harm is documented, time-limited remedies could be paired with investment support; where imported goods supply European factories, open access could continue. The trade-diversion study shows why product-level precision matters, while the vehicle-tariff research shows different effects on buyers. Firms need predictable tests and a chance to contest evidence. The benefit would be security without a needless rise in economy-wide costs, over 2027–2030.
